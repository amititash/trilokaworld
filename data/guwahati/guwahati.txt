## Guwahati: From betel nut trails to urban tales

Close your eyes and step into Guwahati—a city that wears its history like a silken sari. Let's wander through this gateway to Northeast India, where gods dance, tea leaves unfurl, and betel nuts sway.

The capital of Assam, Dispur, lies in Guwahati city, which is also the gateway to Northeast India.

Ringed by the Nilachal Hills, the city stretches along both banks of the broad Brahmaputra river -the mighty river - binds Guwahati's twin souls. On one bank, the old city—its lanes like ancient manuscripts, its temples like folded hands. On the other, the new city—its glass towers reaching for the sky, its dreams as vast as the river's expanse. Bridges connect past and future—the Saraighat Bridge, the Bhupen Hazarika Setu—they're more than concrete; they're arteries.

An ancient seat of tantric Hinduism, with several interesting temples in its environs, Guwahati's air carries the scent of tea gardens. Assam's golden brew—steeped in mist and monsoons—flows through its veins. The tea auctions hum, traders sipping chai as they bid for leaves that cradle stories. And oil—black gold—courses through pipelines, its journey mirroring the Brahmaputra's twists. The city's heartbeat syncs with the rhythm of commerce. This is how Guwahati is now a busy commercial centre for Assam’s tea and oil industries.

Now, let's talk about the betel nut trees—the graceful dancers of Guwahati's fringes. They sway, their emerald leaves catching the sunlight. Betel nuts—small, potent, and wrapped in paan—hold conversations in every market. The vendors, their teeth-stained crimson, hawk their wares. "Betel leaves, sir? A taste of Guwahati!" they call. And if you listen closely, you'll hear the trees whispering: "We've seen empires rise and fall. We've watched lovers meet and part. We're the heartbeat of this market." The city’s outer fringes are dotted with the slender, graceful betelnut palm trees from which Guwahati (literally “Betel Nut Market”) derives its name.

##### Kamakhya Temple

#### Sacred sanctuaries: The divine splendor of Guwahati’s temples

Guwahati isn't just a city; it is a cosmic intersection. Tantric Hinduism weaves its magic here—the kind that transcends rituals and touches the soul. The [Kamakhya Temple], perched atop Nilachal, is a vortex of energy. Devotees climb, their footsteps echoing ancient chants. The goddess Kamakhya—wild, primal, and mysterious—holds court. She's the keeper of secrets, the weaver of desires. And when the monsoons arrive, the temple transforms—a crimson womb, a place of rebirth.

Perched on Nilachal hill, 8 km (5 miles) northwest of the city, the Kamakhya Temple is one of India’s most important pilgrimage destinations. The present structure, with its typically Assamese beehive-shaped shikhara, dates back to the 17th century after the original temple was destroyed by Muslim invaders. According to legend, as a furious and grieving Shiva carried the corpse of his wife, Sati (also known as Parvati), around the skies, parts of her dismembered body fell to the earth. All these sites have been sanctified by major temples. Kamakhya is believed to mark the place where her vagina fell and is therefore said to have special powers associated with energy and creation. As per tantric rituals, a goat is sacrificed here every day and offered to the goddess. There are even giant turtles in the temple ponds that look forward to being fed by visitors. The annual Ambubachi festival, which marks the end of the earth’s menstrual cycle, attracts pilgrims from all over India to be blessed by the goddess.

On Chitranchal hill, in northeast Guwahati, is the [Navagraha (“Nine Planets”) Temple], believed to mark the site of the ancient city of Pragjyotishpur, Guwahati’s old name, which was famous as a centre of astronomy. Beneath its red beehive-shaped dome is a dark chamber with nine lingas representing the nine planets.

Enchantingly located on the lush green Peacock Island in the middle of the Brahmaputra, this 16th-century [Umananda Temple] is also dedicated to Shiva’s wife. The island, swarming with friendly langur monkeys, is an excellent place to stand and watch the river, deceptively slow on the surface but with swift undercurrents.

#### Discoveries and wonders: Journey through Guwahati’s museums and zoo

This interesting [State Museum], just east of the railway station, has fine reconstructions of tribal villages, a comprehensive collection of local handicrafts, and a gallery of medieval stone and bronze sculptures, which were excavated from Ambari, an archaeological site in the heart of the city.

The well-maintained Zoo & Botanical Gardens is in the eastern part of the city. Clouded leopards, hornbills, and, of course, the native one-horned rhinos, can be seen in spacious, moated enclosures. The Botanical Gardens adjoin the zoo.

#### Environs

The Basishtha Temple, 12 km (7 miles) southeast of Guwahati, stands in a pretty spot that marks the confluence of three streams, with a waterfall and groves of trees around it. This is said to be the site of the ashram of the sage Vashishtha, a character in the Ramayana.

Sualkuchi, 32 km (20 miles) west of Guwahati, is a major weaving centre for Assam’s famous golden-hued muga and paat silk. Several houses here have women working at their looms, and they are happy to welcome visitors.

Hajo, 32 km (20 miles) northwest of Guwahati, is a pilgrimage site for Buddhists, Hindus and Muslims. The 16th-century Hayagriva Madhava Temple, on Monikut Hill, is sacred to Hindus and Buddhists, who believe that the Buddha died there. Fine bas-reliefs of scenes from the Ramayana decorate its walls. Below the temple is a pond, home to Hajo’s most famous resident – a giant turtle. On another hill is the Poa Mecca (“Quarter of Mecca”) Mosque, established by an Iraqi prince who visited Assam in the 12th century. A pilgrimage here is believed to be equivalent to a quarter of the piety attained by a Haj pilgrimage to Mecca.

The spectacular temple ruins at [Madan Kamdev] are 50 km (31 miles) northwest of Guwahati. Exuberantly erotic carvings of deities and celestial nymphs lie strewn on a small hillock here. They date from the 10th to 12th centuries when the area was ruled by the Pala dynasty.

Whenever you plan to go to Guwahati, keep in mind that it is a symphony of gods, traders, and the rustle of betel nuts. Let its legends cradle you, and let the Brahmaputra's currents carry your dreams. The ideal time to visit is from October to March.

#### **Quick guide: Places to visit in Guwahati**

- **Where to go in Guwahati for food:** Paradise, Gam’s Delicacy, Michinga, and Kashmiri Nath’s supper club

- **Where to go in Guwahati for temples:** Peacock Island (Umananda Temple) and Kamakhya Temple

Kamakhya TempleDavid Talukdar / Getty Images

For many travellers heading to the Northeast, [Assam] is the customary starting point, its largest city Guwahati, a well-connected entryway into the region. But once you land in Guwahati, you might find it earns its own place on the itinerary, all in keeping with [Assam] ’s “lahe life”, the slow, gentle pace that the state is best known for. Sitting on the banks of the Brahmaputra, the city has just enough to fill a day or two without the pressure of a packed schedule. Temples like Kamakhya and Umananda that double as scenic viewpoints, restaurants and [supper clubs] serving delicious regional thalis, institutions like the State Museum and the Brahmaputra Heritage Centre for cultural context on the region, and short river cruises or sandbar picnics when the weather allows. This list of places to visit in Guwahati also includes day trips, for anyone curious to venture a little further out to tea gardens, wildlife sanctuaries, and national parks–landscapes that show just how ecologically rich and varied this part of Assam can be. **Here are the best places to visit in Guwahati.**

#### **Mahabahu Brahmaputra River Heritage Center**

Overlooking the Brahmaputra from Itakhuli hillock, this heritage centre is built on the grounds of the former Deputy Commissioner’s bungalow, now developed by the Guwahati Metropolitan Development Authority. The 170-year-old bungalow has been restored and houses a series of rooms including the Art Lobby, River Lounge, [Library] Hall, and Attic Room, each focused on the river’s cultural and historical significance. The surrounding pedestrian-only campus includes an open-air amphitheatre, a glass-walled café, a riverside garden with viewing decks, landscaped lawns, and site-specific art installations. Historically, the hill was the base of the Ahom kingdom’s BorPhukan, and the spot from where Lachit Borphukan led the final charge against the [Mughal] army during the Battle of Saraighat. The site includes a craft shop, an art garden, and a dedicated parking area with a connecting overhead walkway. Electric carts run between the main gate and the centre for accessibility. The overall restoration was helmed by architects Smita Makhija and Zia Hussain, its visual identity conceptualised by Guwahati-based artist Manav Dutta Barua.

#### **Restaurants and supper clubs for Assamese food**

Paradise in 1951Paradise

Assamese thali at ParadiseParadise

For a comprehensive introduction to Assamese cuisine, **Paradise** _( [Instagram])_ in Silpukhuri is a good starting point. Operating since 1984, the restaurant is known for its expansive [thaalis] featuring staples of Assamese cuisine. Its most popular dish is the Parampara Thaali, a three-course format that moves from alkaline khar preparations to the sour fish tenga, with stops at duck curry, pigeon meat, and fish steamed in banana leaf. The flavours are pungent, with ingredients like fermented bamboo shoot, mustard paste, and local greens making regular appearances. The Vyanjan Thaali, a more compact spread, includes dal, mashed potatoes, chicken curry, kharoli, and payas. Don’t skip the gooseberry soup or the house-made sorbets of pudina, bel, wild starfruit, served as drinks. Other noteworthy spots to explore are **Michinga** ( [_Instagram_]) for its smoked pork and Naga-style thaalis, and **Gam’s Delicacy**, which offers a good selection of [vegetarian options]. For those looking for a more intimate experience, **Kashmiri Nath** _( [Instagram])_, a food consultant and [supper club] host, has been serving home-style Assamese meals in her residence for the past 10 years. Her menus change, often based on seasonal availability of ingredients and include heirloom recipes that allow guests an in depth taste of traditional Assamese flavours. Meals are by prior booking only.

#### **Assam State Museum**

Founded in 1940 under the leadership of writer and historian Kanaklal Barua, the Assam State Museum is one of the oldest and most comprehensive [museums] in Northeast India. Located next to Dighalipukhuri in central Guwahati, the collection spans archaeology, epigraphy, numismatics, ethnography, and contemporary [art], charting Assam’s political, religious, and material history over centuries. The galleries include stone sculptures from the 5th to 12th centuries, palm-leaf manuscripts, textiles, and a selection of tribal artefacts from across the region. Now under the Directorate of Museums (established in 1983), the institution functions as a key resource for researchers and visitors interested in the layered histories of the Northeast. The museum is open from Tuesday to Sunday between 10am to 5pm, with the exception of Government holidays and the 2nd and 4th Saturday of every month. The entry ticket fee is Rs33 for adults, Rs11 for students, and free for children.

#### **Banks of the Brahmaputra river**

Sandbanks of Brahmaputra riverAssam Bengal Navigation

Beyond being a backdrop to city life, the [Brahmaputra] and its banks are key sites for slow travel in Guwahati. Several operators like Assam Bengal Navigation and Raw Tourism, run short and long-format [river cruises]. They offer access to shifting sandbars, riverine islands like Majuli, home to the 15th-century Vaishnavite monasteries, mask-making traditions, and the Mising community’s weaving and cane crafts. Some cruises include night halts and camping on the seasonal sandbanks complete with bonfires, acoustic sessions, and a full canopy of stars. These activities typically run between October and April, when the water levels are stable and the weather mild. While luxury multi-day cruises head further west or north to [wildlife] sanctuaries and national parks such as Pobitora and Kaziranga, shorter leisure trips around Guwahati provide a quick immersion into the region’s landscape and biodiversity.

#### **Nehru Park**

Located opposite Cotton University in Panbazar, Nehru Park is one of Guwahati’s oldest public green spaces, originally known as Church Field. Spread across 18 bighas, the park sits within the city’s historic academic quarter, an area once home to a colonial-era church and its adjoining cemetery. In 2020, the Guwahati Metropolitan Development Authority undertook a major renovation, adding a waterbody around the Ashoka Stambha, constructing a multi-utility building, and restoring key [sculptures] including that of Swahid Kushal Konwar. A new statue of Gopinath Bordoloi, an independence activist and Assam’s first chief minister, was also installed. While the park has been modernised, the redesign preserved its full canopy cover, maintaining its role as a central and shaded spot for walks, informal gatherings, and weekday quiet.

#### **Srimanta Sankardev Kalakshetra for art exhibits and performances**

Spread across 17.5 hectares in Panjabari, the Srimanta Sankardev Kalakshetra is Assam’s most ambitious state-supported institution dedicated to the preservation and promotion of its diverse artistic [heritage]. Conceived in 1986 by the Government of Assam’s Cultural Advisory Committee, the cultural complex is named after Srimanta Sankardev, the 15th-century poet, reformer, and cultural unifier. The complex includes two fully equipped auditoriums, the 300-seat Sri Sri Madhavadeva International Auditorium and the 1,250-seat Srimanta Sankardev International Auditorium. Along with these, there are also two outdoor performance spaces in the complex: the Rang Ghar Bakori Mancha and an open-air amphitheatre that can accommodate 2,000 spectators. These venues regularly host [traditional music], theatre, and school festivals, particularly in the cooler months. Also on site are a museum, a library, and the Lalit Kala Bhawan art gallery, which has been hosting rotating exhibitions since 2002 by local artists to display their work. Artists and cultural organisations can rent the space for shows, making it a working platform built by the region’s inhabitants. A dedicated study centre for research on Sankardev’s legacy in literature, music, theatre, painting, and philosophy was added in 2016 and holds a growing archive of manuscripts, books, and academic papers. More than a museum or venue, the Kalakshetra functions as a live institution, hosting festivals, performances, and art education programs that reflect the pluralism of Assam and the wider Northeast. The centre is open from 10am to 7pm every day with an entry fee of Rs30 for adults. [_Instagram_].

#### **Dipor Bil**

Dipor BilGetty Images

Among the many destinations for [nature lover] s around Guwahati, Dipor Bil stands out for its ecological importance as well as its proximity, just 8km southwest of the city. A designated Ramsar wetland, this freshwater lake is one of Assam’s richest bird habitats, home to species such as adjutant storks, Baer’s pochard, Pallas’s sea eagle, the Siberian crane, and spot-billed pelicans. In the winter, the area draws migratory birds and also functions as a popular picnic spot for locals. The surrounding Dipor Bil Wildlife Sanctuary is home to populations of Asian elephants, leopards, jungle cats, barking deer, sambar, and porcupines. The sanctuary can be explored by jeep but for those looking to get closer to the water, boat rides, taking [tourists] deeper to the centre of the river, where Gangetic River Dolphins are spotted, can be arranged via the Assam Forest Department’s office on site. Fishing remains a key livelihood for the communities living along the lake’s edge, with the day’s catch often sold in Guwahati markets. While some parts of the bil are under pressure from encroachment and urban sprawl, early-morning visits still offer quiet views of Assam’s wetlands at work.

#### **Peacock Island and Umananda Temple**

Peacock IslandNilomoni Borah / Adobe Stock

A sliver of land marooned in the middle of the Brahmaputra, visible from several points along Guwahati’s riverfront, Peacock Island is the world’s smallest inhabited riverine [island]. It’s home to the 17th-century Umananda Temple, built by Ahom king Gadadhar Singha and dedicated to Lord Shiva. The island’s hill, called Bhasmacala, is associated with Shaivite mythology and draws pilgrims year-round, especially during Maha Shivratri. Visitors can catch regular country boats or ferries from the banks near Uzan Bazar, with the ride taking under 10 minutes. The island’s small footprint makes it easy to walk around, with views across the city and the Brahmaputra. While the temple is the main draw, the setting—dense [trees], langurs, and a ring of water on all sides—makes it equally appealing for a short detour or photo stop while exploring Guwahati.

#### **Kamakhya Temple**

Kamakhya TempleRima Das Mukherjee / Getty Images

Perched atop the Nilachal Hills, about 7km from central Guwahati, the Kamakhya Temple is one of India’s most significant Shakti shrines. While its architecture and setting are striking, the temple is better known for its status as a centre of tantric worship and its celebration of the feminine divine through fertility and cyclical rhythms. The temple’s sanctum houses a natural yoni-shaped cleft in the bedrock, through which a spring flows year-round, marking it as a powerful symbol of creation and regeneration. Rebuilt in 1565 by Koch king Chilarai after being destroyed by Kala Pahar, the current complex includes multiple chambers and shrines. The outer and middle halls feature inscriptions and images of deities, while the inner sanctum remains the focal point of pilgrimage. The temple is dedicated to ten Mahavidyas or forms of the goddess, including Tripura Sundari, Tara, Bagalamukhi, and Chinnamasta, and also houses subsidiary shrines to Shiva and Vishnu in forms such as Kedara and Pandunath. The most important time to visit is during the Ambubachi Mela in June, when thousands gather to mark the goddess’s annual menstruation, a three-day observance during which the temple closes, only to reopen with rituals celebrating fertility and renewal.

#### **Chandubi Lake (1 hour drive)**

Chandubi lakeGetty Images

About 48km from Guwahati, Chandubi is a quiet [natural lake] formed after the 1897 earthquake, set at the base of the Garo Hills near the Assam–Meghalaya border. The drive takes you past tea estates, forested patches, and small villages before opening up to the lake’s still waters and tree-lined banks. A natural lagoon forms part of the landscape, and in the [winter] months, the area draws migratory birds, making it a peaceful spot for birders. Rowboats and country canoes are available for short rides across the lake, and fishing is still a key activity for local communities. With no urban buildup around it unlike Dipor Bil, Chandubi remains largely unspoiled and is best suited to travellers looking for a quick and quiet outdoor day trip.

#### **Sualkuchi Town for silk weaving (50 minute drive)**

For those serious about their textiles, it’s worth venturing out to Sualkuchi, 35km from Guwahati. This riverside town on the north bank of the [Brahmaputra] has been a centre for commercial handloom weaving since the 11th century, when it was developed under royal patronage by the Ahom kings. Today, almost every home in the village houses a taatxaal (hand-operated loom), producing the state’s signature silks: the golden muga, the lustrous paat, and the soft, woolly eri. What sets Sualkuchi apart is its scale and accessibility. Unlike other weaving traditions in the Northeast that are often private or for self-use, Sualkuchi’s looms feed local and national markets. Visitors can observe production from yarn to finished fabric, and buy directly from weavers or small cooperatives in the area. Garments and furnishings here tend to be better priced and more varied than what’s available in city stores. The surrounding village, marked by small temples, fields, and river views, makes the detour feel like a quiet, rewarding break from urban Guwahati.

#### **Kaziranga National Park (3 hour drive)**

Asian elephants walking in Kaziranga National ParkSourav Ganguli / Getty Images

If you’ve only seen the Indian one-horned rhinoceros on postage stamps or wildlife logos, [Kaziranga] is your chance to see them in the flesh. Spread across 430 square kilometres of tall elephant grass, marshes, and semi-evergreen forest, this UNESCO World Heritage Site holds more than 4000 creatures of the once-endangered species. The park straddles the edge of the Eastern Himalayan biodiversity corridor, in Assam’s Golaghat and Nagaon districts, and is one of the most successful conservation stories in South Asia. The park owes its origins to Mary Curzon, wife of the British Viceroy, who, after failing to spot a single rhino on a visit in 1904, persuaded her husband to declare it a reserve. Protection followed, and over the decades, Kaziranga’s population of greater one-horned rhinos has rebounded significantly. But the park is home to a lot more than rhinos. It is a Tiger Reserve , a breeding ground for wild elephants, swamp deer, and water buffalo, and a designated Important Bird Area with migratory species like white-fronted goose, ferruginous ducks, and Asian Openbill storks arriving from Central Asia in the wintertime. Jeep [safaris] take you through the various ranges, its riverine landscape shaped in part by the Brahmaputra. The park is open to visitors from November to April for 2 hour morning (7.30am - 11.30am) and evening (1.30pm to 4.30pm) safaris.The entry fee for adults is Rs100 with added photography costs for those who want to bring a camera.

#### **Pobitora Wildlife Sanctuary (1.5 hour drive)**

Greater One Horned Rhino at Pobitora Wildlife SanctuaryTanvir Parves Ahmed / Getty Images

Just under 50 km from Guwahati, Pobitora Wildlife Sanctuary is often described as Kaziranga in miniature, though its statistics are anything but small. Set in the floodplains of the Brahmaputra in Morigaon district, Pobitora has the highest density of greater one-horned rhinoceroses in India. The sanctuary spans a compact area of grassland and wetland bordered by the Rajamayong hills, making sightings likely even on short [safaris]. The sanctuary is also home to water buffalo, wild boar, and monitor lizards, as well as leopards and langurs in the wooded zones. With over 375 species of birds, Pobitora, like Kaziranga, was also designated an Important Bird Area by BirdLife International back in 2004. The sanctuary is best visited between November and April, when the floodplains are accessible and the grass is short enough for clear views. Jeep [safaris] are the usual mode of exploration, and the compactness of the sanctuary makes it a lot more accessible than Kaziranga.

#### **Manas National Park (2.5 hour drive)**

With [wildlife destinations] like Kaziranga and Pobitora easily accessible from Guwahati, Manas National Park rounds out the trifecta of standout forest reserves in the region. Located along the Bhutan border in the Himalayan foothills, with varied landscapes ranging from savanna areas in the north and grasslands in the south standing on deep deposits of alluvium. Manas is home to several rare and endangered species, including the golden langur, Assam roofed turtle, hispid hare, and the pygmy hog, whose only wild population lives here. In 2024, nine pygmy hogs were released into the park, bringing the total number to 63. The park is also a critical bird habitat, supporting over 450 bird species, including the Bengal florican. Named after the Manas River, the tributary that flows through the park before merging into the Brahmaputra, the sanctuary shares a border with [Bhutan’s] Royal Manas National Park. Rhinos from Pobitora have also been relocated here as part of ongoing conservation efforts. The best time to visit the park is between November to April. The park remains closed from June through September during the monsoon.

#### **Amchong Tea Estate (1 hr drive)**

Amchong Tea Estate

Just 30 minutes from Guwahati, Amchong Tea Estate invites visitors into Assam’s living [tea] heritage. Spread over 1,782 undulating acres (a rarity in Assam’s largely flat tea terrain), the estate produces both CTC and Orthodox teas, known for their strength, clarity, and aroma. Founded in 1958 by Shyamlal Khemka, Amchong has grown from a pioneering [tea garden] into a diversified estate that also cultivates rubber, teak, and black pepper. Visitors can book a day tour to walk through the gardens, try their hand at tea plucking, watch the withering and rolling process at the factory, and end with a fresh harvest tasting over high [tea] for Rs1,499 with shorter packages also available for Rs499. Along with tea focussed tours, the estate also offers rubber tapping demos, pepper farming walks, and forest treks, alongside ATV rides, cycling routes, and stargazing nights. The best time to visit is from March to May, when the weather is pleasant and tea plucking season is in full swing. October to December is ideal for outdoor activities, with cooler days, clearer skies, and excellent conditions for photography. From June to September, the estate is at its greenest during the monsoon, though rains are likely to alter outdoor plans. Amchong’s proximity to Kaziranga and Meghalaya makes it an easy and worthwhile detour for travellers exploring the region.

### 10\. Regional Science Centre: Best Tourist Places in Guwahati City

The Guwahati Science Centre, a beacon of scientific exploration and education, stands proudly in the heart of Assam's capital. Established to ignite curiosity and foster a love for science, this interactive centre provides a dynamic learning environment for visitors of all ages.

### 11\. Srimanta SankarDev Kalakhetra: Best Tourist Places in Guwahati City

The Srimanta Sankardev Kalakshetra in Guwahati, Assam, is a cultural complex dedicated to the medieval saint-scholar and cultural icon, Srimanta Sankardev. This sprawling complex serves as a vibrant hub for the preservation and promotion of Assam's diverse cultural heritage.

The Kalakshetra, named after the revered saint, comprises a museum, an open-air amphitheatre, traditional dance and music studios, and beautifully landscaped gardens. The museum showcases artefacts related to Assam's cultural history, traditional crafts, and the life and teachings of Srimanta Sankardev.

The lush greenery and architectural elements in the Kalakshetra create a serene ambience, inviting visitors to explore and appreciate the rich tapestry of Assamese culture. The complex serves as a center for cultural research, education, and the promotion of traditional arts, making it an integral part of Guwahati's cultural landscape.

One can also check Dr. Bhupen Hazarika Museum established inside the campus in his honour. The Dr. Bhupen Hazarika Museum is dedicated to preserving and exhibiting a diverse array of items connected to the life and achievements of the renowned music icon, Dr. Bhupen Hazarika. Within its walls, the museum proudly displays a collection that includes accolades and recognitions bestowed upon the late Dr Hazarika, alongside personal belongings such as musical instruments, furniture, attire, and more. Additionally, the museum houses significant photographs and noteworthy books that serve as testaments to the substantial contributions and accomplishments of Dr. Hazarika. No photography is allowed inside.

### 12\. Gandhi Mandap: Best Tourist Places in Guwahati City

Guwahati Gandhi Mandap, a towering monument atop the serene Sarania Hills, pays homage to the Father of the Nation, Mahatma Gandhi. This sacred memorial, constructed in his honour, symbolises peace, non-violence, and the indomitable spirit of India's struggle for independence.

The Gandhi Mandap offers panoramic views of the city and the Brahmaputra River, creating a tranquil and contemplative atmosphere. The design of the monument incorporates elements of simplicity, reminiscent of Gandhian ideals, and features a central platform with an imposing bronze statue of Mahatma Gandhi.

Visitors ascend a series of steps, representing the ascent towards truth and righteousness, to reach the main structure. The memorial often serves as a site for prayer meetings, cultural events, and gatherings that commemorate Mahatma Gandhi's teachings and principles.

Surrounded by lush greenery and offering a respite from the urban hustle, the Guwahati Gandhi Mandap serves as not only a historical landmark but also a spiritual destination. It invites reflection on the timeless values propagated by Mahatma Gandhi, fostering a sense of unity, harmony, and reverence for the principles that shaped the course of India's freedom movement.

### 13\. Deepor Beel: Best Tourist Places in Guwahati City

Deepor Beel, a serene freshwater lake located southwest of Guwahati, Assam, is a haven for nature enthusiasts and birdwatchers. Spread over an expansive area, this wetland ecosystem plays a pivotal role in the region's biodiversity and is recognized as a Ramsar site, signifying its global importance for avian diversity. One can also drop by at Balaji Temple on the way to Deepor Bill.

Nestled against the backdrop of the picturesque Rani Hills, Deepor Beel is a vital habitat for various resident and migratory bird species. Birdwatchers are treated to the sight of numerous winged visitors, including migratory waterfowl like the Greater Adjutant Stork, Pallas's Fish Eagle, and the Spot-billed Pelican.

Apart from its avian richness, Deepor Beel is surrounded by lush greenery and supports diverse flora and fauna. The wetland also acts as a natural reservoir, playing a crucial role in flood control for the region.

The Deepor Beel Wildlife Sanctuary, established to conserve this unique ecosystem, offers opportunities for eco-tourism and nature walks. Visitors can explore watchtowers strategically placed around the lake, providing panoramic views of the enchanting landscape.

Deepor Beel stands not just as a picturesque locale but as a vital link in the ecological chain, showcasing the delicate balance between urbanization and environmental conservation in Assam. This natural gem invites all those who appreciate the beauty of unspoiled landscapes and the harmonious coexistence of diverse flora and fauna.

It is located on the outskirts of main Guwahati City so make your plans accordingly. The best time to visit is early in the morning or late evening.

### 14\. Mahabahu Brahmaputra River Heritage Center: Best Tourist Places in Guwahati City

Under the umbrella of the Guwahati Metropolitan Development Authority, the entire hillock, crowned by the Deputy Commissioner's bungalow, and its surrounding grounds have undergone a remarkable transformation into the Mahabahu Brahmaputra River Heritage Center.

This center is devoted to honouring the essence and enduring legacy of the majestic River Brahmaputra. The pathways, shaded by trees, guiding visitors to this exclusively pedestrian campus feature an Art Garden, a 170-year-old Heritage Bungalow, an open-air Amphitheatre, and a contemporary glass-fronted structure serving as a Café. Additionally, the Riverside Garden, adorned with open grassy expanses nestled amidst trees, showcases art installations, purposefully designed activity areas, and observation decks that provide captivating views of the river. The entire landscape is thoughtfully designed to invigorate the senses and offers a serene environment for exploration and appreciation.

Every room you visit has a story to tell and will leave you captivated by its unique narrative, unfolding layers of history, culture, and perhaps a touch of mystery that lingers in the air.

The historical significance of this site as a central hub stretches back to ancient times when this hillock and the adjacent riverfront were known as Itakhuli. This area served as the strategic base camp for the formidable BorPhukan of the Ahom Kingdom. It was on this very hillock that the legendary Lachit BorPhukan orchestrated a crucial battle against the Mughal army. Despite his ailing health, Lachit BorPhukan led a courageous charge with his troops, marking a turning point in the conflict. The audacious attack, witnessed by the flowing waters of the Brahmaputra River, altered the course of the battle, permanently thwarting the Mughal advance. As the campus evolves in the future, there are plans for a memorial dedicated to the memory of Lachit BorPhukan, situated on the far side of the hillock, providing an unobstructed view of Saraighat.

## How to reach Guwahati?

Guwahati, being a major city in Assam, is well-connected by air, rail, and road.

By Air: Lokpriya Gopinath Bordoloi International Airport (GAU) serves Guwahati and is connected to major Indian cities. Airlines operate regular flights to and from Guwahati, facilitating convenient air travel.

By Rail: Guwahati Junction is a prominent railway station with good connectivity to various parts of India. Trains from major cities like Delhi, Kolkata, Mumbai, and others link Guwahati by rail.

By Road: Guwahati is well-connected by a network of national highways. Regular bus services operate from nearby cities, and private taxis are also available. Long-distance travelers often prefer road journeys for the scenic landscapes.

Local Transportation: Within Guwahati, taxis, auto-rickshaws, and cycle rickshaws are common modes of transportation. Car rentals are also available for more flexibility in exploring the city and its surroundings.

### What is the best time to visit guwahati?

The best time to visit Guwahati, the largest city in the Indian state of Assam, is generally during the winter months from October to March. During this period, the weather is pleasant and relatively cooler, with temperatures ranging from around 10 to 25 degrees Celsius (50 to 77 degrees Fahrenheit). This makes it a comfortable time for outdoor activities and sightseeing. The monsoon season, which occurs from June to September, brings heavy rainfall to the region, leading to flooding and challenging travel conditions. The summer months, from April to June, can be hot and humid, with temperatures reaching high levels.

Therefore, for a more enjoyable and comfortable experience, it is recommended to plan your visit to Guwahati during the winter months when the weather is mild and conducive to exploring the city and its surrounding attractions. However, it's always a good idea to check current weather conditions and local events before planning your trip.

# Guwahati

HighlightsGallery

Referred as "the gateway to North-East India", Guwahati is the largest city in the [state of Assam] and North-East India. The city is the second-largest metropolitan area in eastern India after [Kolkata] and one of the fastest-developing cities in India.

Formerly known as Pragjyotishpura, the name Guwahati is derived from the union of two Assamese words - ‘Guva’ meaning Areca Nut, and ‘Hati’ meaning rows, hence meaning "the rows of areca nut trees".

Sandwiched by the river Brahmaputra on one side and the Shillong Plateau on the other, excavations trace the origins of the city to around the 4th century and it was the capital of Assam until the 10th century.

The slow, meandering Brahmaputra River is a focal point of the downtown while the lush hills of the Shillong Plateau provide a verdant backdrop for the suburbs. The capital of the state of Assam, Dispur, is located within Guwahati. Kamakhya Temple, located at the top of the Nilachal Hill and dedicated to Goddess Kamakhya, plays host to thousands of devotees each day.

Travelers usually land at Guwahati Airport and take road journeys to the rest of the destinations in North-East India.

## Top Places to visit in Guwahati

### Kamakhya Temple

One of the _Shakti Peeths_ in the country, the [Kamakhya temple] is situated on the Nilachal hills. It is one of the few natural places to visit in Guwahati that allows you to experience the beauty of the hills.

### Assam State Zoo

Having nearly a thousand animals, this zoo also serves as a botanical garden. Its vast area and picnic spots make it one of the top places to visit in Guwahati.

### Regional Science Center

This is an impressive place that is always a feast for children. While looking for places to visit in Guwahati, you must keep this on your list.

### Sankardev Kalakshetra

With a museum and an open-air theatre, the Sankardev Kalakshetra falls within the charming places to visit in Guwahati. The place also houses traditional artefacts and manuscripts.

### Guwahati Planetarium

If you are an astronomy fan, then don’t forget to spend some time here. Its prime location gives easy access to other prominent places to visit in Guwahati.

## Best time to visit Guwahati

### Summer

Even though summers last from April to May, the city enjoys a moderate climate. This makes it ideal to explore the [places to visit in April] in Guwahati during this time.

### Monsoon

From June to October, the city receives moderate to heavy rainfall. Despite that, it is suggested to set out for [places to visit in july] in Guwahati.

### Winter

It is ideal to see places to visit in Guwahati in December as the weather remains mostly pleasant from October to March. Also, Bhogali Bihu is celebrated during this time.

## Places to Visit in Guwahati with Family & Friends

### Umananda

Situated in the heart of the Brahmaputra river, this temple is a must-visit in places to visit in Guwahati with friends. One has to reach here via a ferry boat.

### Kaziranga National Park

Although it’s not situated in the heart of the city, the Kaziranga National Park is one of the perfect places to visit in Guwahati with family. It is a world heritage site and also the home to one-horned rhinoceros.

### Pobitora Wildlife Sanctuary

An ideal picnic spot, this wildlife sanctuary falls among the idyllic places in Guwahati. You can see hogs, buffaloes, leopards, etc. here.

## FAQs on Guwahati

### What are the top sightseeing places in Guwahati?

The top sightseeing places in Guwahati are Umananda Temple, Kamakhya Temple, Assam State Museum, Umananda Island, Guwahati Zoo, Pobitora Wildlife Sanctuary.

### How much does a package cost for Guwahati?

### How can I commute within Guwahati?

Guwahati is a major city and commuting within the city should not be much problem. Cabs and auto rickshaws are available, although they may be a little over priced specially if you look like a tourist. Uber and Ola both run in the city, so the easiest way to travel within the city would be use these so that you don't have to worry about haggling. Traveling by the local bus is also an option, but newcomers might find it difficult to find the proper bus - this is definitely the cheapest mode of transport.

[Read More] 

### Can you suggest an itinerary for Guwahati?

**Day 1 -** You will arrive in the City by air or road transport, check in to your hotel and gear up for some sightseeing. Hire a cab from private taxi services such as Prime Cabs and tour around the city. Visit the Kamakhya Temple on top of Nilachal hill. After that, you can visit the Guwahati Planetarium which is located at a distance of half an hour from Kamakhya Temple. The drive to the place is very serene as you can enjoy the peaceful Brahmaputra river seashore drive.

**Day 2 -** Start your day by visiting the Assam state museum, a peaceful drive up in the valley. You can make a quick stop for some snacks with chai. Dighalipukhuri is also a must visit in the morning as you can enjoy the scenery and peacefulness in the early hours. Take a quick cab to the Fancy market and enjoy your day sunning and shopping. While driving back to the hotel, get yourself a quick takeaway from Paradise restaurant for dinner.

**Day 3 -** Catch a local bus the next day to Shrimanta Shankardeva Kalakshetra and explore the rich heritage of Assam. Pay a visit to Ugratara Temple and Bura Jame Masjid for some religious experiences and finish your day with a night spent out in Tera Mayaa on GS road.
